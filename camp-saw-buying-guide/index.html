How to choose a camp saw

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

Choose a camp saw by balancing blade length, weight, and packability against the wood you’ll cut. Folding saws are the best all-around for backpacking, bow saws excel at car camps and basecamps, and pocket chain saws are an ultralight but slower backup. Prioritize a locking mechanism and a comfortable handle; sharpness out of the box matters more than brand hype.

What size saw do I need for camping?

Blade length directly dictates what kind of wood you can handle. For general camp tasks like breaking down wrist-thick branches and processing firewood for a small fire, a blade between 6 and 10 inches is the sweet spot. Shorter blades (under 6 inches) work well for small-diameter deadfall and clearing trail blowdown, but they’ll frustrate you on anything over 2 inches thick. For car camping or group trips where you’re cutting larger logs or even splitting small rounds, a blade in the 12-to-15-inch range gives you real leverage. Just remember that longer blades mean more weight and bulk – there’s a reason most backpacking saws top out around 8 inches. Think about your typical campsite: if you’re mostly collecting already dead, pencil-thick sticks, a compact saw is fine. If you’re often cutting through knotty oak or ash, go longer.

Folding saw vs. bow saw vs. pocket chain saw: which is best?

Folding saws are by far the most popular choice for backpackers and general campers. They pack down safely (the blade retracts into the handle), weigh well under a pound, and cut astonishingly well for their size. The key trade-off is reach: even the longest folding saws can’t match a bow saw for cutting large, wet logs. Bow saws are the workhorses of car camping and bushcraft. They’re inexpensive, accept replacement blades easily, and the open frame lets you cut with two hands, generating more speed and power. The downside is bulk – they don’t fold flat, and the blade is exposed unless you carry it in a sleeve. They’re overkill for a solo backpacker but perfect for a cabin or group trip. Pocket chain saws (those flexible loops of cutting chain with handles) are ultralight and pack incredibly small, making them a tempting choice for gram-conscious hikers. In practice, they’re slower and more awkward to use than a folding saw, and they require a lot of arm motion to saw through a log. They’re a reliable backup or emergency tool, but not a primary cutter for steady camp chores.

What features make a camp saw safe and easy to use?

A reliable locking mechanism is the single most important safety feature. Look for a saw whose blade locks open and closed with a positive click – you don’t want it folding up mid-stroke or accidentally deploying in your pack. Many high-quality folding saws use a metal lock ring or a twist-and-lock mechanism that won’t fail after weeks of use. Handle ergonomics matter a lot. A saw that feels too thin or slippery in your hand will cause fatigue and blisters. Rubberized or contoured grips, or handles with a slight curve that fits the palm, are worth seeking out. Also, check for a blade tension adjustment: some folders let you tighten the blade if it loosens over time, which keeps cuts efficient. A good sheath or blade cover isn’t just for storage; it protects other gear in your pack and prevents accidental cuts. For bow saws, a simple cordura sleeve is standard. For folding saws, a locking blade eliminates the need for a sheath, but many users still prefer one for extra peace of mind.

How do I maintain a camp saw for long life?

Keep the blade clean and dry after every trip. Sap and moisture are the two biggest enemies: sap gums up the teeth, reducing cutting speed, and moisture encourages rust even on coated blades. Wipe the blade with a dry cloth, and if you’ve cut pitchy wood, clean it with rubbing alcohol or a citrus-based solvent. Sharpening a camp saw is trickier than sharpening a knife because of the set of the teeth. Most users get years of use before needing to sharpen, because the blades are cheap to replace. For folding saws, simply buy a replacement blade (they’re widely available and cost only a few dollars). For bow saws, swap out the blade for a fresh one. If you do want to sharpen, use a small triangular file that matches the tooth pitch, and maintain the original angle – it’s tedious but doable. Lubricate the pivot point of a folding saw occasionally with a drop of light oil. This keeps the locking mechanism smooth and prevents that annoying sticking when you try to close the saw. Store the saw in a dry place – never leave it in a damp car trunk or garage.

Are saw teeth count and tooth pattern important?

Yes, but more in principle than in practice for most campers. A higher teeth-per-inch (TPI) count, say 8 to 11 TPI, gives a finer, slower cut that works well on dry, hard wood and leaves a clean edge. Lower TPI, around 4 to 7, cuts faster and is better for green, soft wood but leaves a rougher finish. Most quality camp saws split the difference at 7 or 8 TPI, which handles both reasonably well. Tooth pattern refers to how the teeth are arranged – raker, skip, or alternating. For a general-use camp saw, a standard alternating bevel (every other tooth is set left/right) is the most efficient. Some saws marketed for bone or wood have a raker tooth that clears dust, but that’s overkill for typical camping. Don’t agonize over geometry; a well-made saw from a reputable outdoor brand will have a tooth pattern that just works for the blade length and intended use.

What weight and pack size should I aim for when backpacking?

For backpacking, your goal is to keep the saw under around 8 to 10 ounces (about 225 to 280 grams) and packed length under 12 inches. At that weight and size, a folding saw stows easily in a side pocket or strapped to your pack without adding noticeable bulk or bounce. Many excellent models weigh around 6 to 7 ounces and fold down to 9 inches. If you’re going with a bow saw for a basecamp or canoe trip, expect the frame alone to weigh close to a pound, and the total package with spare blades and a sleeve may push 20 ounces. That’s fine if you’re moving gear by vehicle or paddle, but it’s heavy for a multi-day hike. The takeaway: match the saw’s weight to how far you plan to carry it. A luxury tool is only a luxury if you aren’t cursing it by mile 8.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a camp saw to cut firewood for a campfire?

Yes, absolutely – that’s its primary purpose. A camp saw is ideal for breaking down dead, standing wood into manageable lengths for your fire. For logs thicker than your wrist, a saw is safer and more efficient than trying to baton or break branches by hand.

How do I choose between a folding saw and a pocket chain saw?

Choose a folding saw if you want consistent, fast cuts with less effort – it’s the better primary tool. Choose a pocket chain saw only if weight is absolutely critical (e.g., ultralight thru-hiking) and you’re willing to trade cutting speed and convenience for packability.

Do I need to sharpen my camp saw?

Most of the time, no. Replacement blades are inexpensive and widely available, so it’s easier to swap than sharpen. If you do sharpen, use a small triangular file that matches the tooth pitch and follow the original angle – but expect it to take patience and practice.

Which camp saw is best for car camping?

A bow saw in the 12-to-15-inch blade length is the standard choice for car camping. It cuts quickly, blades are cheap to replace, and the larger frame lets you tackle bigger logs without struggling. Pair it with a simple blade cover for safe storage in the car.

Are there any safety tips I should know before using a camp saw?

Always lock the blade in the open position before cutting, and keep your free hand clear of the cut line. Use a sawing motion that lets the saw do the work – don’t force or twist it. When carrying, secure the blade closed or in its sheath to prevent accidental deployment.

How do I store a camp saw when not in use?

Store the saw clean and dry, with the blade either retracted (for folding saws) or covered with a sheath (for bow saws). Keep it out of direct moisture – a dry gear closet or inside your pack with a silica pack is ideal. Never store it wet or caked in sap.

In shortA good camp saw is one of the most rewarding tools you can bring: it makes fire prep faster, safer, and far less frustrating than snapping or hacking at wood. Whether you’re an ultralight hiker or a weekend car camper, choose a saw with a reliable lock, a blade length suited to the wood you actually encounter, and a comfortable handle you can grip all day. With just a little care, your camp saw will outlast many trips and become a trusted part of your outdoor kit.
